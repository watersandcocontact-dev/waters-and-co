# Premium Isn't About Price - It's About Positioning

## One-paragraph thesis
Premium is a positioning outcome, not a price point. A ~$2 product can be perceived as luxury if everything around it — design, content, partnerships, retail placement, athlete/community association, and storytelling — signals premium, while the price stays anchored to what the category's consumer already expects to pay. Mackay's playbook: pick a high-velocity category, change only 1–2% of the familiar format (but make that 1–2% genuinely better), design packaging for the shelf it will sit on rather than for aesthetics, win an anchor retail customer yourself before hiring intermediaries, keep the SKU range brutally narrow until each product earns expansion, own your formulation IP, and use founder-led selling and content as leverage while racing to make the brand independent of the founder. Underlying it all: unit economics and supply-chain visibility must be built for scale *before* entering mass retail, because retail punishes stockouts and weak velocity, and nobody — retailer or consumer — is loyal.

## Core concepts & frameworks

### 1. Premium through positioning, not price
Premium does not have to be expressed through price point. It is created through positioning, content, brand partnerships, where the product is sold, and where the brand shows up. You can be the cheapest-feeling-expensive item in a category ("the champagne of X") — priced within category norms but styled, distributed, and storied like a luxury good. Corollary: you generally *cannot* price 2–3x above what the category's consumer is trained to pay (especially in low-ticket categories), so premium perception must come from everything except the price tag.

### 2. "Premium at scale" — the central tension
Very few brands hold premium positioning while achieving mass scale. Reference models discussed:
- **On Running** — worn by both dentists and elite athletes; flagship stores on premium streets; multi-billion revenue with premium positioning intact.
- **Red Bull** — lifestyle/extreme-sport empire (F1 team, athletes) wrapped around ~$10B/year of ordinary carbonated beverage.
- **Nespresso** — arguably mediocre coffee, but premium content, education, packaging, and stores make a ~$90 machine feel luxury at mass-market prices.
- Apparel caveat: in clothing, ubiquity kills desirability ("everyone's wearing it, you don't want it anymore"); in consumables, the opposite — the more people consume it, the more others want it. Category dynamics change how scale affects premium perception.

### 3. Strong brands remove confusion; they don't add noise
In a cluttered category, the premium move is clarity, not decoration. Simplify what the product is, what it is not, and its unique selling points so a shopper can decode it in milliseconds.

### 4. Design for where the product will sit ("shelf-back design")
A minimal, beautiful direct-to-consumer aesthetic can fail in physical retail, where shoppers decide in fractions of a second. Mackay's first can looked like a classic minimal D2C can and underperformed; a redesign — bold colored flavor rim, explicit callouts (no sugar, no caffeine), clear flavor identification — with the *identical liquid* tripled sales. Lessons:
- Data informs design; rebrand iteratively (three rebrands in ~300 days is fine).
- Regulatory/mandatory elements (barcode, UPC, required callouts, origin) consume ~35% of a package — design within what's allowed, not what's ideal.
- Moving from D2C to retail is like moving from top-of-funnel branding to direct response: the package itself must sell.

### 5. The 1–2% rule (build within the category)
When entering an established category, don't reinvent the wheel. Keep the familiar format (standard can size, standard price band) so consumers stay comfortable, and change only the 1–2% that matters — for Mackay: design, positioning, and the liquid itself. Product quality is non-negotiable: it must be craveable, because repeat purchase is the entire game in both D2C and retail.

### 6. Category-velocity selection
Choose categories that turn fast. ~86% of Americans buy a cold beverage daily; grocery beverage can turn 20 units/store/week where other categories turn 1. Same door count at 20x the velocity is a wildly different business. High velocity raises retailer expectations (you must earn the shelf), but it's also where nine-figure quarters become possible.

### 7. Retail = distribution marketing; sell-through > sell-in
Door count is vanity. The KPIs retailers and operators actually track:
- **Units per store per week (UPSW)** — the core success measure.
- **Dollars per store per week** — what your shelf earns the retailer.
- You "win retail every 30 days": data arrives, you adjust price, add promotion (e.g., 2-for-$4), and iterate.
- Trade promotion mechanics: the brand typically funds 50–100% of any promotional discount, often ~12 weeks/year. Promotion drives trial; the goal is that after returning to everyday low price (EDLP), your base velocity has permanently jumped. You must have the margin structure to fund this and still make money.

### 8. Retail-readiness test (two questions)
Before entering mass retail, be able to answer yes to:
1. **Unique economics:** if velocity explodes, can you sell at wholesale into mass retailers and still hit target gross/contribution margin (or at worst break even while costs fall with volume)?
2. **Supply-chain visibility:** do you know exactly what happens to cost, capacity, and lead time at each scale milestone?
Don't enter retail until the business is built for the scale success would force on it. Retail severely punishes failure: poor sell-through gets you delisted, and re-entry is very hard.

### 9. Channel strategy: go where the customer already buys
- The traditional CPG ladder (farmers market → premium natural grocers → Whole Foods → conventional) is no longer mandatory; convenience/accessibility now dominates consumer behavior. If your ambition is to displace the category giant, go where the giant sells (mass retail), not where the artisan brands cluster. (Example cited: David protein bar skipping the natural channel entirely to chase the mainstream candy-bar shopper at drugstores.)
- Match each product to its natural channel: mainstream formats (canned drinks) belong in grocery aisles next to the incumbents; niche/specialist formats (running gels) belong online/D2C/Amazon where that customer actually shops. Don't try to drag a new behavior into a channel.

### 10. Distributors vs. brokers ("box movers" vs. "sales agents")
- **Distributors** move boxes; they will never sell your product for you. Margins ~15–25%. They consolidate logistics (you ship to one point) but cost margin. They're also gatekeepers — a "necessary evil" that can unlock customers you can't reach, and you likely need both direct and distributor relationships eventually.
- **Direct distribution** preserves margin (better for the P&L; reinvest in price or marketing) but carries heavy logistics/supply-chain burden.
- **Brokers** are commissioned sales agents with many client brands; they prioritize whoever is already winning. Never assume a broker is actively selling for you.
- **Sequence:** win the anchor retail customer yourself first. Only then do distributors/brokers want your business — and you hand it to them from a position of leverage with explicit expectations.

### 11. Founder-led sales
The founder's job is to sell — to the team, the customer, the buyer, the investor. Do not outsource your zone of genius: a broker in a Walmart meeting will never match the founder in that meeting. Even with a sales team, the founder should show up to build buyer relationships and learn what success looks like, until scale genuinely forces delegation. "The best brands in the world — the founder is still out there selling."

### 12. The two-pitch model for retail
- **Pitch 1 (no data):** differentiation in the category + incrementality you bring the retailer + the D2C audience/community you will push into their doors + evidence of cultural momentum (events, communities, content sizzle). Position yourself as a thought partner solving the retailer's problem (e.g., "you have thousands of marathon runners in your store's city and no product for them — let us be that answer").
- **Pitch 2 (with data):** "We sell X units/week at $Y in comparable stores — this could be you." Radically easier; retailers respond to fear of missing measurable revenue.

### 13. The D2C-to-retail flip
Build a strong D2C/subscription base (Cadence: ~65% subscription on site), then at retail launch deliberately redirect that audience into stores ("go buy it at Target") — driving the early velocity data that retail success depends on. D2C becomes a marketing and trial engine; mass retail is where unlimited scale lives.

### 14. SKU discipline: "all your wood behind one arrow"
- Fewer SKUs beat bloated ranges. Concentrating all capital on one SKU compounds purchasing power and drives cost down; spreading across products/manufacturers/terms spreads you thin.
- Heuristic: don't launch the next SKU until the current one reaches ~$10M revenue. Start with one SKU/one flavor; "earn the right" to expand when retailers ask for options.
- Retailers do want 2–3 options at launch — a small flavor set is fine; a sprawling range is not.
- Keep related products at the same manufacturer where possible; every additional co-manufacturer multiplies management burden.

### 15. Stage-gate innovation process
Every proposed product passes a formal gate: size the prize; identify manufacturer; margin requirements; MOQ; cost to the business; lead time; channel strategy — then **underwrite with an anchor customer** (don't press "go" on production until a retailer has committed to launch it). Exception: a founder veto (~once/year) for either (a) a company-changing innovation nobody's watching, or (b) a small-prize product that grounds the brand in its mission and serves the core customer (the 80/20: feed the core ICP and cement positioning vs. pure volume).

### 16. Product architecture: pillars × channels
Define the customer's journey/day (e.g., pre/during/post activity → "core, race, recover") and launch assets under each pillar, matched to the channel where that buyer shops. Concentrate ~90% of effort on the scale product (the one with mass-retail potential); use the rest as high-margin, high-subscription D2C innovation that funds the business and creates a halo (someone tries the niche product at an event, then buys the flagship at retail — cross-sell both ways).

### 17. Own your formulation IP
Most brands white-label a manufacturer's stock formula — fast to market, but you own nothing except your logo. Alternative: hire an independent formulator project-based, pay for development and samples, then transfer the technology to a co-manufacturer to run. You own the IP — which matters enormously at acquisition time. Ground formulation decisions in data/clinical evidence (e.g., dosing set from actual physiology like average sweat-loss studies, validated by a credentialed sports-science hire), and don't dumb down the spec to match weak incumbents — let the market catch up and use the superior spec as the differentiation story to retailers.

### 18. Founder-led content — and the race to exit it
- Biggest regret from the first company: not documenting the journey. Founder content is near-free customer acquisition (a phone and time) with very high ROI early.
- Use the founder's audience as fuel, but keep the brand separable: don't name the company after yourself; keep the founder mostly off the brand's feed; measure attribution of customers by source so you know the dependency. Goal: the brand must survive the founder stepping back from the spotlight (not from operations — from the *face*).
- Early on the founder should be deep in the details (down to individual social posts) to transmit taste and standards to a new team; delegate as the team internalizes the vision.

### 19. Athlete/ambassador tiering and the "cringe filter"
- **Tier 1:** a few elite athletes who ground authenticity, credibility, and performance positioning — high cost, low direct ROI beyond credibility. (Elite performance without audience doesn't sell product; distribution/reach amplifies everything.)
- **Tiers 2–3:** volume influencers with codes/content/ads — these drive ~99% of D2C revenue.
- Hard brand filter regardless of ROI: "Do I want to *be* this person?" If an influencer doesn't look/feel like where you want the brand positioned, no amount of ROI justifies them.
- Leverage: build an internal content engine and shoot professional content *for* your athletes — you're giving them agency-grade assets and distribution, which buys better deals.

### 20. Collaboration rules
Good collabs = non-competitive categories + same ICP + each side does what it does best + categories you've explicitly committed never to enter yourself (so the partnership can't curdle into competition). Occasional exception: overlapping categories with genuinely different audiences, where each side wants the other's demographic.

### 21. Manufacturer relationships and MOQ leverage
- Distinguish must-have from nice-to-have specs; the de-risked version of a premium package (e.g., sleeved vs. printed cans at ~1/10 the MOQ) buys you into the game.
- Manufacturers field dozens of pitches daily; your job is to sell them your trajectory so they bet their time on you. Show up in person; 15 minutes face-to-face outperforms weeks of email. (The speaker admits he overstated his traction to his first manufacturer to get taken seriously — framed by the hosts as "painting the big picture"/self-belief. Treat this as a candid confession of a common tactic, not a recommended practice: credibility, in-person effort, and a believable vision are the transferable parts.)

### 22. Sold-out is a channel-dependent signal
- D2C limited drops: selling out is good marketing (scarcity psychology).
- Daily-consumption products and B2B/retail: selling out is a *failure* — it breaks the daily habit you're selling, hands customers to competitors (nobody is brand-loyal), and in retail gets you replaced on shelf ("if you're not on, I'll put your competitor on"). Demand planning is among the most important functions in a physical-product business.

### 23. Speed as the small-company weapon
"We won't win on capital or resources; we'll win on speed." Leadership sets the pace: compress every timeline ("a week? what does 3 days look like?"), and compounded across 100 decisions a month it's a decisive advantage — roughly until ~$100M scale, after which other factors dominate. Top performers want pressure; those who crack under honest deadlines self-select out. (Reference: *Amp It Up*, Frank Slootman.)

### 24. Hiring: generalists → spikes
- Early stage (0→~$10M): hire hungry generalists who do everything.
- Scaling ($10M+): hire "spikes" — world-class at one thing (demand planner, creative director, paid media), per Ramp founder Eric Glyman's model.
- Hire for intensity/appetite over resume; add experience via paid mentors/experts (e.g., booking calls with operators who've done the exact thing) rather than hiring expensive veterans who managed large teams and can't operate lean.
- Interview probes: "You quit in 3 months even though we're crushing it and you're well paid — why?" (the answer reveals values/fit; dodging is a red flag). "When did you join that company?" (joining pre-$50M is a different job than joining at $500M; ask team size behind their wins).
- Retention = impact + clarity ("clarity creates velocity"): show people the goal and how they tangibly move it; make them feel "13 feet tall"; when adding hires, let incumbents keep the parts they're passionate about. People leave founders, not companies.

### 25. Fundraising principles
- Never skimp on a lawyer or accountant — term-sheet fine print is the difference between controlling your business and not. Founders are routinely ignorant of basics (strike price, liquidation waterfall, preferred terms).
- A SAFE is "a discount on tomorrow": sell the forward build (doors × SKUs × velocity → next-round valuation), not trailing multiples.
- Capital raised is not a success metric; the business must make sense with or without the money. Raising from a position of not needing it is the strongest position.
- Prefer operator-investors ("war room, not board room") over pure capital allocators, who make big decisions on an inch of information one hour per quarter. Vet investors socially (dinners, events, exposure to your network) — "not all money is good money."
- Show, don't pitch: invite investors to feel the community live (events, stores, race weekends).

## Step-by-step playbooks

### Playbook A: Positioning a low-priced product as premium
1. Do obsessive in-aisle research: watch real shoppers, note pickup speed, competitor claims, price points, and white space.
2. Anchor price inside the category's expected band (you can't charge 2–3x category norms in low-ticket goods); premium must come from everything else.
3. Pick the 1–2% to change: keep the familiar format/size, improve design, positioning, and the product itself.
4. Make the product genuinely excellent — craveability drives the repeat purchase every other lever depends on.
5. Build the premium signal stack: minimal-but-clear design, elevated content/storytelling, credible athlete/expert association, high-status partnerships, and presence at culturally premium moments (events, marathons, pop-ups).
6. Coin a positioning phrase that names the paradox (e.g., "$2 luxury," "the champagne of [category]") and verify the factual claim behind it.
7. Study premium-at-scale models outside your category (footwear, coffee, energy drinks) for how they hold both ends.

### Playbook B: Designing packaging for retail
1. Design for where the product will sit — the shelf, not the moodboard.
2. Map mandatory/regulatory elements first (~a third of the package may be untouchable); design within constraints.
3. Optimize for the milliseconds decision: what is it, what flavor, what it does NOT contain (key USP callouts), from arm's length.
4. Launch, then read sell-through data per store; treat packaging as an iterating variable (multiple rebrands early is normal).
5. If velocity disappoints, test packaging changes with the identical product before touching anything else — packaging alone can multiply sales.

### Playbook C: Entering mass retail
1. Pass the readiness test: (a) wholesale unit economics survive scale (target 50%+ gross margin in CPG); (b) full supply-chain visibility at each scale milestone.
2. Know the money math: consumer shelf price − ~50% retailer margin = your sell-in price; subtract distribution + shipping to get your margin over COGS. Budget 20–30% of revenue for trade spend.
3. Build Pitch 1 (no data): category differentiation, incrementality, your D2C audience as a trial engine, community/cultural proof, thought-partnership on the retailer's own trend problem.
4. Win an anchor customer yourself — founder in the room, not a broker.
5. At launch, flip your D2C audience into stores to seed velocity.
6. Manage the 30-day cycle: read UPSW and $/store/week, adjust price/promotions, fund trial promotions (expect to pay 50–100% of the discount), and confirm base velocity steps up after each promo returns to EDLP.
7. Use Pitch 2 (with data) to expand: "here's our velocity in comparable stores; this could be you."
8. Only after the anchor is won, hand logistics to distributors (accept 15–25% margin) and layer brokers — with explicit expectations, KPIs, and accountability, and never assuming they're selling for you.
9. Never stock out in retail: build demand planning (pipeline + velocity + cash + inventory + lead times) as a core function.

### Playbook D: Product-line expansion (stage-gate)
1. Define your product architecture: customer's day/journey → 2–4 pillars → channel per product.
2. Concentrate ~90% of resources on the single scale SKU until it hits a hard revenue KPI (e.g., $10M) — then "earn the right" to expand.
3. For each proposed SKU, run the gate: size the prize → manufacturer → margin requirement → MOQ → cash cost → lead time → channel strategy.
4. Underwrite the launch with an anchor customer commitment before triggering production.
5. Reserve a rare founder veto for mission-grounding or company-changing products with small immediate prizes.
6. Keep new SKUs with existing manufacturers where possible.
7. Use niche/high-margin D2C products to fund the business and halo the flagship (deliberate cross-sell both directions).

### Playbook E: Owning your formulation
1. Decide deliberately: white-label (fast, no IP) vs. custom formulation (slower, you own the moat).
2. If custom: contract an independent formulator project-based; pay for time and samples.
3. Ground the spec in data — published studies, testing, credentialed expert input; set dosages from evidence, not incumbent norms.
4. Take the finished formula to a co-manufacturer as a technology transfer; the co-man runs it but never owns it.
5. Use the superior, evidence-backed spec as your retailer differentiation story; expect the market to catch up to your spec rather than dumbing down to match it.
6. As you scale, bring formulation in-house with full-time developers.

### Playbook F: Founder-led brand building
1. Document the journey from day one — a phone is enough; the ROI on founder content is time, not money.
2. Keep the founder's public focus narrow (2–3 themes) so the audience is sticky and on-ICP.
3. Track customer attribution by source (founder, influencers, word of mouth, podcasts) so you know your dependency and can manage it down.
4. Keep the brand's own channels almost founder-free; the brand must be able to outlive the founder-as-face.
5. Stay in the creative details early (posts, copy, campaigns) to transmit standards; let the team run as they internalize the vision.
6. Build tiered talent: few tier-1 credibility athletes + volume tiers of revenue-driving influencers, all passed through the "would I want to be this person?" brand filter.
7. Build an internal content engine; give partners agency-grade content as deal leverage.
8. Choose collabs by the rules: non-competitive category, same ICP, mutual halo, categories you've sworn off entering.

### Playbook G: Early-stage hiring
1. Stage-match the profile: generalists to ~$10M, spikes beyond.
2. Screen for intensity, pressure appetite, and velocity over resume length.
3. Ask the reveal questions: "Why did you quit in month 3 despite everything going right?" and "When did you join [brand-name employer], and how big was the team doing the work?"
4. Buy pattern recognition through paid mentor/expert calls instead of overpriced veteran hires.
5. Set pace from the top; give people clarity on the goal and their lever ("clarity creates velocity").
6. Empower juniors with a sandbox and real resources ("make people feel 13 feet tall"); protect their passion areas when redistributing work.
7. Gift/institutionalize the culture texts you run on (e.g., *Amp It Up* to every new hire).

### Playbook H: Raising capital (founder-favorable)
1. Hire a strong early-stage lawyer and accountant before signing anything; learn the vocabulary (strike price, waterfall, preferred terms, SAFEs).
2. Raise from your immediate network first; price fairly so early backers get real upside — don't over-price the round.
3. Pitch the forward build: doors × SKUs × POs × velocity → the 12-month valuation the discount is against.
4. Prefer operators-turned-investors; vet every check socially and be willing to reject money on red flags.
5. Let investors experience the business live rather than only reading the deck.
6. Treat capital raised as cost, not achievement; keep the business viable without the round.

## Prerequisites & warnings

**Prerequisites**
- A genuinely excellent product — every other lever assumes repeat purchase.
- 50%+ gross margin structure at wholesale prices before considering mass retail.
- Supply-chain visibility: costs, MOQs, lead times, and capacity at each scale milestone.
- Cash for inventory (inventory is cash) and for trade spend (20–30% of revenue).
- A demand-planning capability before retail commitments — retailers don't grant extensions.
- A defined ICP and the discipline to build innovation and content for that ICP only.
- Founder willingness to personally sell and to be deep in details early.
- Category homework: obsessive competitive/aisle research and knowledge of who has and hasn't disrupted the category.

**Failure modes the speakers call out**
- Designing packaging for aesthetics instead of the shelf → beautiful cans that don't turn.
- Confusing sell-in with success → "in 10,000 doors" turning 1 unit/week is a dying business.
- Entering retail before economics/supply chain are ready → one bad sell-through cycle gets you delisted, and re-entry is very hard.
- Stockouts in B2B/daily-consumption → broken habit, lost shelf, customers permanently switched (nobody is loyal).
- Believing brokers/distributors will sell for you → "don't go to sleep thinking that guy is doing anything on your behalf."
- Outsourcing the founder's zone of genius (sales, brand) to agencies/brokers.
- Launching too many SKUs / spreading capital across manufacturers → costs stay high, focus dissolves.
- White-labeling and believing you own something → at exit you own only a logo.
- Hiring on resume/experience → veterans who managed big teams often can't operate lean or endure the pace; also, joining a brand at $500M is not the same skill as building it to $50M.
- Pricing far outside category norms in low-ticket categories → consumers won't follow.
- Chasing influencer ROI past the brand filter → short-term revenue, long-term positioning damage.
- Skimping on legal in fundraising → term-sheet terms that cost you control.
- Taking money from non-operator investors who make major decisions on minimal information; ignoring red flags because you're focused on the check.
- Believing your own "sold out" hype — D2C scarcity theatrics don't transfer to retail.
- Overstating traction to partners (the speaker admits doing this with a manufacturer): it can work but stakes your credibility entirely on delivering; the safer transferable lesson is conviction + in-person effort + a believable forward story.

## Generic action checklist
- [ ] Spend real hours where your customers buy (aisle, app, marketplace): record pickup behavior, price points, claims, and white space in your category.
- [ ] Write down the category's expected price band and position your price inside it; list the non-price levers (design, content, partnerships, placement) that will carry premium perception.
- [ ] Define the 1–2% you will change versus the category standard — and verify the rest of the offer stays familiar.
- [ ] Pressure-test product quality with real target users until repeat purchase intent is obvious.
- [ ] Audit your packaging/landing page for the "milliseconds test": can a stranger identify what it is, the variant, and the two key claims at a glance? List the mandatory/regulatory elements first and design around them.
- [ ] Run a packaging/presentation A/B against sales data before concluding anything about product-market fit.
- [ ] Compute your full wholesale waterfall: shelf price − channel margin (assume ~50%) − distribution/shipping − trade spend (assume 20–30%) → confirm 50%+ gross margin survives.
- [ ] Write the two-question retail-readiness memo: unit economics at 10x volume, and supply-chain costs/lead times at each milestone.
- [ ] Build Pitch 1 (differentiation, incrementality, community you'll drive into the channel) and define what data will power Pitch 2.
- [ ] Identify the one anchor customer that unlocks your category, and have the founder personally pursue it before engaging any broker or distributor.
- [ ] Instrument the core velocity metric for your channel (units and dollars per outlet per week, or the equivalent) and set a 30-day review-and-adjust cycle.
- [ ] Write your SKU discipline rule (e.g., "no new product until the flagship hits $X") and a stage-gate checklist (prize size, margin, MOQ, lead time, channel, anchor customer) for every proposed launch.
- [ ] Decide deliberately between white-label and owned IP for your core offering; if owned, contract independent development and keep the IP.
- [ ] Ground your key product spec in verifiable data and turn that evidence into your differentiation story.
- [ ] Map each product to the channel where its buyer already shops; kill plans that require dragging customers into unfamiliar channels.
- [ ] Start documenting the founder journey now (phone is enough); track customer attribution by source; keep the brand's channels able to stand without the founder's face.
- [ ] Build a two-tier talent/partner program: a few credibility anchors + volume revenue drivers, all passed through a written brand filter, all managed with KPIs.
- [ ] Stand up demand planning: pipeline commitments + current velocity + cash + inventory + lead times reviewed on a fixed cadence.
- [ ] Adopt the hiring stage rule (generalists → spikes), add the two interview reveal questions, and budget for paid expert calls instead of premature senior hires.
- [ ] Before any fundraise or major contract: engage a proper lawyer and accountant, learn the key terms, and build the 12-month forward model that justifies the price.

## Best suited for

**Maps well to:**
- Consumer packaged goods (food, beverage, supplements, personal care) — the playbook's home turf, especially challenger brands entering established, high-velocity categories.
- Any physical product moving from D2C into physical retail/wholesale — the readiness tests, margin math, pitch sequence, and demand-planning discipline transfer directly.
- Low-ticket, high-frequency products where premium perception must be built without premium pricing.
- Founder-led brands in lifestyle/community-adjacent spaces (fitness, hobby, wellness) that can use content, events, and tiered talent for distribution.
- Service or digital businesses can borrow the positioning half (premium via clarity, placement, content, and partnerships rather than price; remove confusion; niche ICP focus; founder-led selling; "earn the right" product expansion; hiring stage rules; fundraising hygiene).

**Maps poorly to:**
- Businesses where scarcity is the model (limited drops, luxury goods, appointment-based services at capacity) — the "sold out is failure" and mass-accessibility logic inverts.
- High-ticket/considered-purchase categories where pricing above the category *is* the premium signal and buyers expect long sales cycles.
- Pure software/SaaS on the mechanics (MOQs, trade spend, distributors, shelf velocity have no direct analog) — use only the positioning, hiring, and fundraising layers.
- Regulated professional services where advertising, claims, and partnership structures are constrained — the "paint the big picture to partners" tactic in particular is inappropriate where accuracy duties apply.
- Apparel/fashion at scale without adaptation — the video itself notes ubiquity can *destroy* desirability in apparel, the opposite of consumables.
