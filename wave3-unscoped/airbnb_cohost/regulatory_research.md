# Australian Short-Term Rental (STR) Regulatory Landscape — Research for Airbnb Co-Hosting Business

**Research date:** 30 July 2026
**Prepared for:** Solo entrepreneur evaluating a paid "co-hosting" service (guest communication, pricing, cleaning coordination, listing optimisation) in exchange for a percentage of nightly revenue.

> **Re-verification note:** This is one of the fastest-moving regulatory areas in Australian property law right now — several states (NSW, VIC, WA, ACT) have introduced new registers, levies, or planning rules in the last 12–18 months, and QLD is actively considering a state-wide scheme. Council-level rules change even more often than state rules. Treat everything below as a snapshot as of July 2026, confirm current details directly against the primary sources linked before acting on them, and **get state-specific advice from the relevant real estate/property regulator (and ideally a lawyer) on the licensing question before taking a single paying client.**

---

## 0. THE HEADLINE ISSUE: DOES CO-HOSTING FOR A FEE REQUIRE A REAL ESTATE LICENCE?

**Short answer: in most Australian states, yes — almost certainly, once money changes hands.**

Every state and territory has a real estate/property/land agents act that defines a "real estate agent" (or "property agent", "land agent", "resident letting agent") in broadly the same way: **a person who, for reward, acts for another person in the leasing, letting, or management of real property.** Short-term/holiday letting has repeatedly been confirmed — by regulators and in court/tribunal decisions — to fall inside that definition. Simply being "just a co-host" who doesn't hold the lease or collect rent in your own name does **not** appear to create an exemption; the trigger is "acting for reward in the letting/management of someone else's property," which is exactly what a percentage-of-revenue co-hosting fee is.

**Bottom line for the business model:**
- In **QLD and VIC**, operating a fee-based co-hosting business without a licence is a confirmed, enforced legal breach (fines, prosecutions on record). This is a **hard blocker** in those states absent a licence.
- In **NSW and WA**, the statutory language and regulator guidance point the same way, though enforcement examples were less directly found for WA in this research — treat as effectively the same hard blocker.
- In **SA, TAS, ACT, NT**, the statutes use materially the same "acts for another, for reward, in letting/managing property" language, so on a plain reading the same licensing obligation almost certainly applies — but this research did not turn up STR-specific enforcement precedent or explicit regulator FAQ guidance for those four jurisdictions. **Do not treat the absence of a found enforcement case as evidence it's unregulated** — it may simply be under-enforced or newer territory. Confirm directly with each regulator (SA: Consumer and Business Services; TAS: CBOS; ACT: Access Canberra; NT: Licensing NT) before operating there.
- A path that is **very likely to be safe everywhere** without a licence: working purely as an **unlicensed assistant/subcontractor under a property owner's own direction** (e.g., paid hourly/flat-fee for specific tasks like answering messages using the owner's own account, at the owner's direction, with the owner retaining control) — but the moment the arrangement looks like "I manage your listing and take a cut of revenue," most states will treat that as agency work requiring a licence. The safest structurally compliant model in licensing-strict states (QLD, VIC, NSW, WA) is either (a) get licensed (Certificate IV in Real Estate/Property Services, ~6–12 months part-time, ~$500–$2,500 depending on state/provider, plus licence application fees ~$100–$500), or (b) work as a licensed agent's representative/employee under a principal who holds the licence, or (c) restrict the service to genuinely owner-directed task execution rather than owner-delegated management/letting authority — and get that structure reviewed by a lawyer, because the line is fact-specific and regulators have prosecuted people who thought they were "just helping."
- Many successful Australian Airbnb management companies (Houst, MadeComfy, Aircabin, AirKeeper, etc.) operate as **licensed real estate agencies** specifically because of this — that is itself evidence the licensing requirement is treated as real and commercially unavoidable at any scale in the eastern states in particular.

## 1. New South Wales (NSW)

**Registration:** All STRA properties must be listed on the NSW Government STRA Register before advertising or accepting bookings. Registration fee ~$65, annual renewal ~$25. Hosts must display their STRA Property ID on listings.

**Day caps (unhosted/whole-home):**
- Greater Sydney: non-hosted STRA capped at **180 days/year**.
- Hosted STRA (host lives on site): **no cap**, 365 days/year.
- **Byron Shire**: tightened in Sept 2024 to a **60-day cap** for unhosted STRA across most of the LGA, with two mapped 365-day exception precincts (Byron Bay town centre and Brunswick Heads). Illustrates how far council/local rules can diverge from the state default.

**Safety requirements:** Interconnected smoke alarms, evacuation diagrams, and adherence to a mandatory Code of Conduct. Payments/deposits handled by an agent are trust money under the *Property and Stock Agents Act 2002*.

**Strata/body corporate:** Owners corporations can pass a **by-law by special resolution** (s137A, Strata Schemes Management Act 2015) banning short-term letting of a lot **unless it is the owner's principal place of residence**. This followed *Estens v Owners Strata Plan 11825*, where NCAT initially found an outright ban invalid, prompting the legislature to explicitly authorise a (qualified) ban. Practical implication: many NSW apartment complexes now have valid by-laws banning or restricting non-owner-occupied STR — always check the by-laws/strata roll before onboarding a unit.

**Licensing:** See Section 0 — required if operating "as an agent"/"carrying on business," which a paid co-hosting service would be.

## 2. Victoria (VIC)

**Levy (not quite a registration scheme, but similar compliance burden):** The **Short Stay Levy** (State Revenue Office) took effect 2024–2025: **7.5% of the total booking fee** for stays under 28 consecutive days. Hosts/platforms not booking exclusively through platforms that remit the levy must register directly with the Commissioner of State Revenue. Properties earning <$75,000/year lodge annually (first return due 30 Jan 2026); ≥$75,000/year lodge quarterly.

**Day caps:** No explicit statewide day-cap found for unhosted STR in this research (Victoria's control mechanism is the levy + owners corporation powers below, rather than a hard day cap like NSW). Some councils (e.g. Mornington Peninsula) have their own local laws with additional obligations (e.g. a "Designated Contact Person" who must respond within 2 hours) — check the specific council.

**Body corporate/strata:** From **1 January 2025**, owners corporations can register a **rule banning short-stay accommodation** (stays <28 days) entirely, via special resolution requiring **75% of total lot entitlements/votes**, except where the lot is the owner/lessee's principal residence. VCAT can impose penalties up to $250 per breach of a registered rule. This is a significant risk factor for apartment-based clients — always confirm OC rules before signing a property.

**Licensing:** See Section 0 — confirmed requirement (Estate Agents Act 1980), with a prosecuted precedent for exactly this business model (managing multiple Airbnb/Stayz listings for owners without a licence).

## 3. Queensland (QLD)

**State-level registration:** **None currently** — as of mid-2026 Queensland has no state STR register, no state levy, and no state night cap. The Queensland Government is reportedly considering a unified state-wide registration scheme and standardised code of conduct, so this may change.

**Local council variation (illustrative, not exhaustive):**
- **Gold Coast:** No council register or night cap, but the correct commercial rates category applies, planning approval (Material Change of Use) is needed for unhosted STR outside tourism zones, and **Local Law 16.1 mandates $10M broadform public liability insurance**.
- **Noosa Shire:** Stricter — mandatory local approval with **annual renewal** for all STR properties, plus a Code of Conduct requiring hosts to have processes for guest nuisance/complaints.
- Given the lack of a state scheme, **council rules must be checked individually for every property/LGA** a co-host might take on — this is a genuine operational complexity for a multi-property co-hosting business spanning several councils.

**Licensing:** See Section 0 — the clearest, most explicitly confirmed hard blocker of any state. *Property Occupations Act 2014* s26 requires a real estate agent licence or a Resident Letting Agent licence for anyone letting/managing property for others for reward, explicitly including Airbnb/Stayz management. Two real prosecution examples were found (a Gold Coast operator fined ~$1,800; a QLD operator managing 16 properties facing court). The Resident Letting Agent licence is a narrower, faster qualification path than the full real estate agent licence and may be the more proportionate option if focusing purely on letting/management rather than sales — but it is still a licence, not a loophole.

## 4. Western Australia (WA)

**Registration:** Under the *Short-Term Rental Accommodation Act 2024*, **all STRA (hosted and unhosted) had to register on the WA STRA Register by 1 January 2025**, before advertising or taking bookings. A registration number must then be displayed prominently on all listings/ads.

**Planning approval / day caps:** From **1 January 2026**, unhosted STR in the **Perth metropolitan area** operating more than **90 nights/year** requires planning (development) approval from the local council. Hosted STR is exempt from development approval statewide. Regional-area requirements vary by council — check directly.

**Safety requirements (STRA Regulations 2024):**
- Smoke alarms per Building Regulations 2012 Pt 8 Div 3 — must not be expired, must have tamper-resistant 10-year battery if not mains-powered, or be ≤10 years old if no expiry shown. Non-compliance penalty up to **$5,000**.
- Pool barriers must have been inspected within the 4 years prior to registration/renewal (Building Regulations 2012 Pt 8 Div 2).

**Insurance:** Platforms are required to give operators written notice that their **ordinary home/contents insurance may not cover liability, defence costs, or first-party loss** while the property is used for STR — i.e., WA explicitly flags the standard "personal insurance doesn't cover this" gap that applies Australia-wide (see Section 7).

**Licensing:** See Section 0 — *Real Estate and Business Agents Act 1978* defines "real estate transaction" broadly enough (leasing, letting, and collecting payments for use/occupation, for reward) to capture STR management for a fee; this reads the same way as the confirmed QLD/VIC/NSW positions, though no specific WA STR prosecution was found in this research.

## 5. South Australia (SA)

**Registration:** **No statewide STR registration or licensing scheme** as of this research — SA relies on individual local councils, and there is no state law preventing strata/apartment owners from short-term letting.

**Proposed changes (not yet in force):** Adelaide City Council and others have floated: charging STR properties **commercial council rates** (~20% higher than residential), a **permit system** with registration/fees, and **night caps**. None of these were confirmed as enacted state-wide as of this research — **re-verify before relying on "no rules" in SA**, especially for Adelaide, Glenelg, and Victor Harbor, which were specifically named as high-demand/high-scrutiny areas.

**Licensing:** *Land Agents Act 1994* requires registration for land agents, sales representatives, property managers, and auctioneers acting for others for reward (property manager registration mandatory since 2019). This statutory pattern matches the other states, but this research did not find SA-specific guidance or case law confirming STR/Airbnb co-hosting is treated the same as conventional letting — **flagged as uncertain; confirm directly with Consumer and Business Services SA** before treating SA as lower-risk than QLD/VIC/NSW.

## 6. Tasmania (TAS)

**Planning permit / "visitor accommodation":** Short stay accommodation is termed "visitor accommodation" under the Tasmanian Planning Scheme. A **planning permit is generally required**, unless the **"home-sharing" exemption** applies — i.e., the dwelling is the host's main residence and either (a) only let while the host is away, or (b) no more than 4 bedrooms let while the host is present. Buildings over 200m² may trigger a change-of-use assessment requiring a building surveyor.

**Data reporting:** Under the *Short Stay Accommodation Act 2019*, booking platforms must report listing data to the Director of Building Control quarterly — a transparency/enforcement mechanism rather than a cap.

**Day caps:** No specific statewide day-cap for STR found in this research beyond the home-sharing exemption's structural limits — re-verify with the State Planning Office / local council, as this varies (e.g. Hobart City Council has its own visitor accommodation planning provisions).

**Licensing:** *Property Agents and Land Transactions Act 2016* licenses real estate agents, property managers, and property representatives who act for reward. Statutory pattern matches other states; **no TAS-specific STR/Airbnb enforcement guidance found — flagged as uncertain, verify with Consumer, Building and Occupational Services (CBOS) Tasmania.**

## 7. Australian Capital Territory (ACT)

**Registration:** **No mandatory state-wide licensing/registration scheme** for STR properties as of this research (unlike NSW/WA). There is, however, a declaration process distinguishing "hosted and excluded accommodation."

**Levy:** The *Short-Term Rental Accommodation Levy Act 2025* introduced a **5% levy** on STR bookings of up to 28 nights, administered similarly to Victoria's levy.

**Day caps:** Hosted STR (host on-site) can operate year-round unrestricted; **non-hosted STR is capped at 180 days/year**.

**Licensing:** *Agents Act 2003* licenses real estate agents who act for another to sell, buy, **lease, or manage property**, with multiple licence classes (including a specific conditional licence for managing an owners corporation). The general definition reads consistently with other states, but **no ACT-specific guidance or case law confirming STR/Airbnb management triggers this was found — flagged as uncertain, verify with Access Canberra.**

## 8. Northern Territory (NT)

**Registration:** **No specific state-wide STR regulatory scheme** — the lightest-touch jurisdiction of the eight. Regulation is primarily through ordinary planning/development approval where a property operates "at commercial scale" or causes amenity impacts, assessed locally (e.g. by Darwin City Council). Electronic platforms (Airbnb, Vrbo, Booking.com) must still report short-stay transactions to the ATO for tax compliance purposes, same as everywhere else in Australia.

**Day caps / safety rules:** None found specific to STR at the NT or Darwin level in this research — re-verify with Darwin City Council and other NT councils directly, as this is the least-documented jurisdiction of the eight and rules could exist locally without being well-indexed.

**Licensing:** *Agents Licensing Act 1979* requires a licence for a person who, for reward, acts for another in the sale, purchase, leasing, or letting of real property, including managing properties and arranging rentals. Statutory pattern matches other states; **no NT-specific STR enforcement or guidance found — flagged as uncertain, verify with Licensing NT / NT Consumer Affairs.** Note that light state-level STR regulation does **not** imply light agent-licensing regulation — these are separate statutory regimes.

## 9. Insurance (cross-cutting, all states)

- **Ordinary home/contents insurance frequently does not cover STR use** and can be **voided** if the insurer treats short-term letting as a change of use to a commercial/boarding-house risk without disclosure. This was explicitly flagged as a regulatory notice requirement in WA and is a general market risk everywhere else.
- **Airbnb's AirCover for Hosts** provides host liability cover, but only up to **US$1 million per incident** — well below the **$10–20 million public liability** commonly required by Australian strata by-laws, council local laws (e.g. Gold Coast's $10M requirement under Local Law 16.1), and general commercial norms.
- **Practical implication for a co-hosting business:** clients should be advised (in writing, as part of onboarding) to hold a dedicated STR-specific insurance policy disclosing the short-term letting use, plus adequate public liability. A co-host who fails to flag this is exposed to liability themselves if something goes wrong and the owner's cover turns out to be void — this should be built into the standard client agreement/checklist, and the co-host should also independently confirm their own business insurance (professional indemnity/public liability) given they are handling other people's assets and guest safety.

## 10. Typical co-hosting fee structures in the Australian market

Based on multiple industry sources (Houst, Hostaway, Grow Host, Lodgify, MadeComfy, and dedicated Australian co-hosting quote services):

- **Overall market range: ~10–30% of booking/nightly revenue**, with most full-service offerings clustering around **15–25%**.
- Rough tiering seen across sources:
  - **10–15%**: light-touch / "communications only" (guest messaging support, no on-the-ground work)
  - **15–20% (up to ~20–22%)**: standard/mid-tier full co-hosting (messaging, pricing, cleaning coordination, listing optimisation) — this is the closest match to the service described in this brief
  - **20–25%+**: premium/bespoke full-service or luxury properties, sometimes including in-person check-in/out, 24/7 issue handling, more intensive guest experience work
- Fee basis matters and varies by operator: some charge % of **gross booking total** (incl. cleaning fees, extras), others only % of the **nightly rate**. This affects like-for-like comparison and should be made explicit in any pricing model/contract.
- Full third-party **property management companies** (which hold real estate licences and take on more liability/scope) tend to sit at the higher end (~20–25%+) compared to informal/independent co-hosts.

**Implication for positioning:** A 15–20% fee for a mid-tier "manage everything except being physically present" service is consistent with the market. Given the licensing exposure identified above, factor the cost/time of getting licensed (or partnering with a licensed agent) into the pricing model in QLD, VIC, NSW and WA specifically.

## 12. Recommendations / open questions before launching

1. **Treat QLD and VIC as confirmed no-go without a licence.** Do not take paying co-hosting clients there without either (a) obtaining the relevant licence (Resident Letting Agent in QLD is the faster/narrower path; full Estate Agent's licence in VIC), or (b) operating as a representative/employee under someone who already holds one.
2. **Treat NSW and WA the same way** given the strength of the statutory language, even though this research surfaced regulator guidance rather than a specific prosecution for WA.
3. **Before operating in SA, TAS, ACT, or NT, get a direct written answer from the relevant regulator** (CBS SA, CBOS TAS, Access Canberra, Licensing NT) on whether fee-based STR co-hosting is treated as agent's work. Don't assume "less regulated STR rules" means "less regulated agent-licensing rules" — those are two separate laws, and the agent-licensing side looks consistently strict across every state examined.
4. **Get a lawyer to review the actual service contract/scope of work** against the licensing threshold in each target state — the line between "unlicensed operational assistant working under owner instruction" and "unlicensed person acting as an agent" is fact-specific and has caught people out (see QLD/VIC prosecutions) even when they believed they were just helping.
5. **Build strata/OC and council-level checks into client onboarding** — a property with a valid by-law ban (increasingly common in NSW/VIC apartment buildings) or a council permit requirement not yet obtained is a compliance liability for both the owner and the co-host, regardless of licensing status.
6. **Build an insurance disclosure/checklist into onboarding** — confirm the owner holds STR-appropriate insurance with adequate public liability (well above Airbnb's US$1M AirCover ceiling) before listing, and separately obtain the co-host's own business insurance.
7. **Re-verify all of the above close to launch** — QLD's state-wide scheme is reportedly under active consideration, SA's council-rate/permit proposals were not confirmed as enacted, and NSW/VIC/WA/ACT have all changed rules within the last 12–18 months. This document should not be relied on beyond a few months without a refresh.
